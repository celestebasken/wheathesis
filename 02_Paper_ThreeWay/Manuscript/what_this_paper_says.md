# What this paper says

---

## The question

Mycorrhizal fungi are usually described as helpful to crops — they extend the root system and
improve water and nutrient uptake. But whether they help or cost the plant depends on
circumstances, and almost everything we know about that concerns *physical* circumstances: how
much water, how much phosphorus, how much light.

I asked whether the plant's **neighbors** matter too. Specifically: does having a legume growing
next to it change whether the fungal partnership helps or costs wheat when water runs short?

## What I did

I grew wheat in a greenhouse under three watering levels, with and without mycorrhizal fungi,
either by itself or alongside a fava bean. Sixty pots, five per combination. At harvest I weighed
what each plant had produced and separated it into roots, shoots, and grain.

## What I found

**Wheat grown alone, with fungi, under severe drought produced far less grain.** Not a small
difference — a collapse.

**The plant itself did not grow less.** Total biomass was essentially unaffected. The plants grew just as much,
but invested into stems and roots instead of seed.

**This wasn't because the plants were bigger.** Larger plants normally make more grain, and the
inoculated plants under severe drought were the larger ones — yet they made less. So it's a real
change in how the plant divided its resources, not a side effect of size.

**A fava neighbor prevented it.** In mixed pots, the collapse didn't happen. The two stand types
were indistinguishable under normal and moderate watering, and diverged only under severe drought.

**The fungi also cost the fava.** Where wheat was inoculated, its fava neighbor grew smaller — but
that suppression disappeared under severe drought.

## What it means

The fungal partnership seems to pay off for wheat when water is adequate: that's visible in how
much it suppresses the neighboring fava. Under severe drought the payoff disappears while the cost
remains. Growing alone, wheat covers that cost out of its grain. With a legume neighbor, it
doesn't have to.

The broader point is that the plant's biological neighborhood — not just the physical environment —
determines whether this partnership is a benefit or a burden. For farming, it suggests that
inoculating a monoculture carries a yield risk in a drought year, and that intercropping might
hedge it. That's a hypothesis for field testing, not a recommendation.

## What I can't say

**I don't know the mechanism.** I tested whether the fungus shifted from actively exchanging
resources to simply storing them for itself. It hadn't. Colonization stayed intact in both amount
and kind while the plant's grain output collapsed. The most likely remaining explanation is that
the fungus delayed the plant's development so grain filling was cut short, but I didn't record
flowering or senescence dates and cannot test it.

**I can't separate "fava" from "fewer wheat plants."** Mixed pots held half as many wheat plants
plus one fava, so the neighbor treatment changed two things at once. The comparison I care about —
whether the drought effect differs between stand types — is much less affected by this than a
simple mono-vs-mixed comparison would be, but it can't be fully ruled out without a control I
didn't run.

**This is a greenhouse, in pots, one variety, one season.** It's a claim about mechanism and
context, not a prediction about field yields.

## Where it might go

An ecology journal, framed around the idea that mycorrhizal outcomes depend on the biotic
neighborhood, with a short section on agricultural implications. *Plant and Soil* or *Mycorrhiza*
are the natural homes. A short-format communication built around the single central finding is a
legitimate alternative to a full-length paper.
